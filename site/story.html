The skin conditions research forgot

Keratosis pilaris, the rough little bumps many people have on their upper arms, is very common. This story counts how often it has been studied on ClinicalTrials.gov, next to other common skin conditions. It also looks at what does get tested in those trials, and at some of the newer ideas being studied.

1 A common condition, hardly studied

Counting the registered trials…

Keratosis pilaris, often shortened to KP and sometimes called “chicken skin”, causes small, rough bumps around hair follicles, most often on the upper arms, thighs and cheeks. The bumps can be red, itchy or bothersome, and they often last for years.

These are counts of studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. They do not measure how much a condition matters, and some studies are registered elsewhere or not at all. A study that names several of these conditions is counted under each.

5 Listings that have not been updated

A listing counts as not updated here when it still says it is recruiting, but its planned primary completion date passed more than six months before the data was fetched. A date given only as a month or a year counts from the end of that month or year. Usually that means nobody has updated the record, not that anything went wrong. If you are thinking about joining a trial, it is a reason to check with the study team that it is still open.

    How the numbers were counted

    Every number on this page is computed in your browser from the data files listed below.

    • Each condition is a search of ClinicalTrials.gov for that condition name. A trial that names more than one of these conditions is counted under each; where the story adds the conditions together, each trial is counted once.
    • “Recruiting” means the listing says recruiting, not yet recruiting, or enrolling by invitation.
    • A trial’s start year is the start date on its listing. Some are planned dates in the future.
    • Intervention types and phases are as each listing states them. Device, procedure and behavioural studies usually have no phase.
